The Gap Between Research and Practice

Far from practice
Practitioners report that the problems studies address are often far removed from the work of schools
Hard to apply
Findings are often difficult to interpret and to apply in a specific district
Too late
The decision has often been made by the time the findings are ready
Research-practice partnerships are “long-term collaborations between researchers and practitioners that leverage research to address persistent problems of practice.” Coburn, Penuel, & Geil (2013), in Henrick, Cobb, Penuel, Jackson, & Clark (2017), p. 1

Three Types of Partnerships

Research alliance
Works with one district or region on its questions

Example: Consortium on Chicago School Research (University of Chicago, Chicago Public Schools, community organizations); the Regional Educational Laboratories
Design research partnership
Researchers and practitioners design, study, and improve a change to teaching and learning

Example: MIST project, with four large urban districts, on middle-grades mathematics
Networked improvement community
Several organizations work on a shared problem: they gather evidence, test new ideas, and share what they learn across the network
Many partnerships now combine features of more than one type.
Henrick, Cobb, Penuel, Jackson, & Clark (2017), pp. 2–3

Twelve school districts work on the same problem: supporting students in their first year of high school. Each district tests small changes in a few schools, the districts share results every month, and changes that work in one district are tried in others. Which type of partnership is this?

  1. A research alliance
  2. A design research partnership
  3. A networked improvement community
  4. A traditional research study
Hypothetical example.

Partnership Research Compared With Traditional Research

Traditional research Research-practice partnership
Who chooses the question Usually the researcher Researchers and practitioners together
Timing Findings when the study is done Findings ready when the decision is made
The district’s goals Any benefit is a bonus A main purpose of the work
Sharing findings Journal articles Interpreted together; briefs, infographics, webinars
Judging success Publications Publications alone do not capture it
Henrick, Cobb, Penuel, Jackson, & Clark (2017), pp. 1, 8–11

Five Dimensions of Effectiveness

Quality of the partnership
1  Building trust and cultivating partnership relationships

2  Conducting rigorous research to inform action
Results of the partnership
3  Supporting the partner district or school in achieving its goals

4  Producing knowledge that can inform improvement efforts more broadly

5  Building the capacity of researchers, practitioners, and their organizations to do partnership work
Progress on the first two appears to be critical for progress on the other three. Table 1 lists indicators for each.
Henrick, Cobb, Penuel, Jackson, & Clark (2017), p. 4; Table 1, pp. 24–25

Examples of Indicators

1  Trust
Researchers and practitioners routinely work together.

Warning sign: meetings repeatedly canceled or rescheduled.
p. 5
2  Research
The partnership studies a problem before testing solutions.

Alliance researchers used descriptive analyses to understand how deep and complex an issue was.
p. 9
3  District goals
Crediting a partnership on principals’ feedback with test-score gains is “almost certainly flawed” when other initiatives are running. Track the steps in between.
p. 12
Henrick, Cobb, Penuel, Jackson, & Clark (2017)

Continuous Improvement

In networked improvement communities
Members use the tools of improvement science to gather evidence, test new ideas, and share what they learn.
Henrick, Cobb, Penuel, Jackson, & Clark (2017), p. 3
Proving Ground’s goal for its partners
Make evidence-based continuous improvement part of “the ordinary course of business” in districts and state agencies.
Hersh (2021)

Steps in an Improvement Cycle

1
Define the problem and set an improvement goal
2
Identify root causes
3
Identify possible changes that address the causes
4
Choose one change to try
5
Design the change for the people who will use it
6
Plan how it will be carried out and how progress will be tracked
7
Run a pilot
8
Use the pilot’s evidence to decide: stop, scale, or adapt
9
Reflect on the results against the improvement goal
The steps repeat until the improvement goal is met. The general pattern is often called Plan-Do-Study-Act.
Steps from Hersh (2021), optional reading

One Improvement Cycle in a Hypothetical School

Step A middle school
Problem and goal More sixth graders are missing school than last year; reduce absences by spring
Root causes Absences by grade, day, and student group: many come from students absent again soon after a first absence
Change Advisory teacher calls home after a second absence in a month
Pilot Two advisory groups for six weeks; absences tracked in all advisory groups
Decision Absences fell in the pilot groups and somewhat in the others: adapt and run again with more groups

What Descriptive Data Can Show

Can show
Where a problem is concentrated
Which students it affects
Whether a measure rose or fell during a pilot
How the pilot groups compare with groups that did not get the change
Cannot show on its own
That the change caused the improvement

Other initiatives run at the same time (Henrick, p. 12), and districts that join a network may differ from those that do not (Hersh)
Describe the pattern accurately, compare with groups that did not get the change, and state what the data can and cannot support.
