This course builds both. Your dissertation and the final project ask you to do both.

Regression with Controls

Review: Regression allows us to explore relationships between any two variables

regress x1rscalk1 x12sesl 
   x1rscalk1 | Coefficient  Std. err.      t
-------------+------------------------------
     x12sesl |   4.842       0.093    52.05
       _cons |  37.840       0.076   500.69

 N = 14,088        R-squared = 0.161
A one-unit increase in SES is associated with 4.84 more points on the kindergarten reading assessment. SES explains about 16% of the variation in reading scores.

Start from one predictor before adding more. The line is the regression: the coefficient is its slope.

Two things to read off the output. The slope, 4.84 points per unit of SES. And the R-squared, 0.161, which says SES accounts for about 16 percent of the variation. That leaves 84 percent unexplained, which is the opening for everything that follows.

The Language Gap

What happens to a coefficient when you add another variable to the model.
Children whose families primarily speak a language other than English scored lower on kindergarten reading. Families who primarily speak another language are also more likely to be in poverty. So when we compare reading scores by language, some of what we are seeing may be about poverty.
We are back in the ECLS-K file you used for Modules 6 through 8 and for your capstones.

Reload the setup rather than re-teaching it. This is review, and the point of the review is the operation they are about to see in a published table.

Open the conditional means table first so the regression is a compression of something they can see: reading by language within poverty group.

Adding a Control

No control
regress x1rscalk1 nonengl
Coefficient on nonengl: −3.28
Non-English-primary children score about 3.3 points lower.
Controlling for poverty
regress x1rscalk1 nonengl poverty
Coefficient on nonengl: −1.97
Holding poverty constant, the gap is about 40% smaller.
The number moved because language and poverty are correlated. Comparing children within the same poverty category gives a different answer than comparing all children at once.

Run both live rather than reading the slide. The slide is the anchor they look back at.

The interpretation to land, in their own words before you give it: some of what looked like a language gap was a poverty gap.

Point at the N and connect it forward to the practice: adding a variable can change who is in the sample.

Interpreting Controls

What it does
Compares observations that are alike on the control variable, so the coefficient reflects the relationship within those groups rather than across them.
What it does not do
Make the comparison causal. It handles the factors you measured and included, and does nothing about the factors you did not.
This is the same distinction you worked with last term: the confounders you can observe, and the ones you cannot.

Short slide, but it is the one that keeps their language honest for the rest of the semester. Every critique this term comes back to the second box.

Model Building

Add one variable at a time and watch the SES coefficient. Predict before each model.

SES barely moves. Gender is close to unrelated to SES, so holding it constant changes nothing. Language is related to SES, so it moves the coefficient a little. Compare the language coefficient here, −0.545, with the −3.28 it had on its own.

This is the esttab table from the demo file. One column per model, which is how published papers present it and how their written assignments should.

Run it live and have the room call out the next variable before you type it. Ask for a prediction each time: up, down, or steady.

The payoff is the last line. On its own the language coefficient was -3.28. With SES and gender in the model it is -0.545. Most of what looked like a language gap was SES.

Practice

Different outcome, different controls. Work in your breakout group, in Part 4 of saturday_demo_regression.do. Everyone runs the code on their own machine.

Regress the math score on SES. Write down the coefficient and the N.
2
Add female. Before you run it, predict: does the SES coefficient go up, down, or stay put?
3

Two questions to answer as a group: what happened to the SES coefficient as you added controls, and why does the N change from model to model?

Pick one person to report back when we return.

Make them commit to a prediction out loud or in chat before each run. Predicting is the part that builds intuition; watching output scroll by does not.

Why SES holds steady
SES is close to unrelated to gender and only weakly related to disability, so holding them constant changes little. Compare the language example, where adding a related variable did move the coefficient.
Why the N drops
2,682 children are missing disability, and Stata drops any observation missing a variable in the model. Adding a control can change who is in your sample, not only what you hold constant.

Take the reporters first, then put this up.

The gender result is worth pausing on: female is not significant in model 2, p equals 0.156, but is significant in model 3. A coefficient can change when a control enters. That is the argument for reading the whole sequence rather than only the last column.

Part 3
Reading a Results Table

The Paper

Claessens, Duncan, and Engel (2009), Economics of Education Review.
The question
Which kindergarten skills predict fifth-grade achievement: academic skills, attention, or socioemotional skills?
The data
ECLS-K, the 1998–99 kindergarten cohort. A different cohort from the file you have, same study design.
The sample
21,260 children at baseline. Missing data reduce the analysis samples to between 8,000 and 9,400.

Mark the word “impacts.” We come back to whether the design earns it.

Note the cohort difference so nobody expects the paper’s numbers to match what they just ran.

Flag the sample drop now and come back to it: they start with 21,260 and analyze 8,500. Who is missing, and does it matter?

The word “impacts” in the abstract is worth marking. We come back to it at the end of the segment.

The Model Behind Table 1

This is the regression the authors estimate, as it appears in the paper.

ACHi5th
The outcome: fifth-grade reading or math. Reading is columns 1 to 5 of Table 1, math is columns 6 to 10.
ACAD, ATTN, SE
The three kindergarten skill sets, measured in the fall. These are the rows of Table 1.
FAM, CHILD
Family and child controls. Adding these is what changes from one column to the next.
Every number in Table 1 is one of these coefficients, estimated on a different set of controls.

Put this up before the table so the table has something to attach to.

Read the equation left to right in words: fifth-grade achievement for child i is a constant, plus a coefficient on each of the three kindergarten skill sets, plus family and child controls, plus an error term. Point out that this is the same structure as the regression they ran an hour ago, with more terms on the right-hand side.

The subscripts are worth one sentence. The i is the child, and iFK means that variable is measured on child i in the fall of kindergarten. Subscripts tell you the unit and the timing, which is most of what they encode.

The connection to make, and the reason this slide is here: the betas are what the table reports. A published table is a way of showing a set of coefficients from an equation like this one, and reading the table means knowing which coefficient is in which cell.

Do not spend long here. Ninety seconds, then move to the table.

Table 1 as Published

This is what you will actually meet in a paper: ten columns, small type, and the notes doing a lot of work at the bottom. We will take the left half apart on the next slide.

Put this up and let them sit with it for a moment before you say anything. It should look dense, because it is.

Ask what they notice first, and where their eye goes. Most people go to the stars. Then point out that the column headers are the real key, and that the note at the bottom tells you the variables are standardized.

The Coefficient Across Columns

Kindergarten reading skill, predicting fifth-grade reading.
Column Coefficient What is in the model
(1) .503 Kindergarten reading alone.
(2) .310 Adds family background controls and classroom fixed effects, but no other kindergarten skills.
(3) .096 Adds the other kindergarten skills, including math. No background controls.
(5) .073 Everything: other skills, background controls, classroom fixed effects.
The big drop is from .310 to .096, and that comes from adding the other kindergarten skills. Adding the full set of family background controls after that moves the coefficient very little, from .096 to .073.

This is the payoff of the segment. Two things to draw out.

First, they did this forty minutes ago: their language coefficient went from −3.28 to −1.97 when poverty came in. Same operation, published version.

Second, and less obvious: which controls matter is an empirical question, not a rule. Here the demographic controls barely move the coefficient, and the other skills do almost all the work. Students often assume the demographic controls are the important ones. The paper makes this point itself.

Writing About Regression Results

How the authors describe the table in the manuscript. A useful model for your own writing.
“Turning first to the full-control models in columns 5 and 10, it can be seen that fall of kindergarten math, reading and attention skills are predictive of subsequent reading achievement, while early reading skills are not predictive of subsequent math achievement.”
“The coefficients from our full-control models, shown in columns 5 and 10, are similar to those in columns 3 and 8, which suggest that the addition of the full set of family background variables … changes the coefficient estimates very little.”
Three things to copy: they name the exact columns, they state the direction and the size, and they write “predictive of” rather than “causes” or “improves.”

Read the first quote aloud, then ask what they notice about the verb. “Predictive of” is doing careful work: the design cannot support a causal verb and the authors know it.

Contrast with the abstract, which uses “impacts.” Worth asking which one they would have written.

Point out the column references. A reader can check every claim against the table, which is the standard for their own written work this term.

Merge Diagnostics

Unmatched is not the same as wrong
In step ③, 73 rows do not match. Those are the grade 4 rows, and there is no 2024 grade 4 file. Reading _merge and explaining the number is the skill.
When a merge fails
Try merging on district alone, without grade, and watch it fail. Then use duplicates tag distid, gen(dup) to find out why. This is how you will actually debug your own merges.
One caution about these files: the 2024 mean scale scores are on a different scale from 2023. Compare the two years using Percentage in Level 3 or Above, not the scale score.

Do the failed merge deliberately and slowly. Watching a merge fail and then diagnosing it is worth more than four successful merges.

The scale change is in the student README too, but say it out loud.
