A Timeline

History and Part 4 draw on lecture notes by Thomas Dee, Stanford University.

The Coleman Report

The mandate
Civil Rights Act of 1964, Section 402: survey the lack of equal educational opportunity within two years.

About 650,000 students and teachers in more than 3,000 schools.

Framework borrowed from agricultural production functions: school inputs on one side, achievement on the other.
Coleman et al. (1966)
The finding
Once students of comparable family background were compared, measured school resources showed little association with achievement.

Summary that stuck: family background matters more than schools.
Reanalysis (Mosteller & Moynihan, 1972): a single cross-section cannot identify the effect of school resources. Better designs and longitudinal data would be needed. (Module 8: selection.)

From NIE to IES

National Institute of Education, 1972–1985
Founded to resolve empirical debates in education as NIH had in medicine.

By the late 1970s: no Salk vaccine; education research seen as low quality and behind the rest of social science.

Abolished in 1985.
Institute of Education Sciences, 2002
Education Sciences Reform Act: a mandate for “scientific research” in education.

Large investment in randomized trials; the What Works Clearinghouse launched the same year.

Since: better data and methods for causal inference; more emphasis on relevance to practice (Module 12).

PlayPosit Question 1

A state education agency compares third-grade reading scores across its districts. Districts that spend more per pupil score higher, but after accounting for family income the difference nearly disappears. Which conclusion is best supported?

  1. Spending has no effect on reading achievement
  2. Spending would raise achievement if it were targeted to low-income districts
  3. Family income causes the difference in achievement
  4. The comparison cannot separate the effect of spending from the other ways high- and low-spending districts differ; answering the question requires a design from Module 7 or 8
Hypothetical example.

The What Works Clearinghouse

Reviews studies of education programs against a published handbook (version 5.0) and rates each study.
Meets Standards Without Reservations
A randomized trial with low attrition.
Module 7
Meets Standards With Reservations
A randomized trial with high attrition, or a quasi-experimental study that shows the groups were alike at the start.
Module 8
Does Not Meet Standards
No comparison group, or no evidence of baseline equivalence.
Rated studies feed intervention reports and practice guides. The criteria are the Module 7 and 8 questions: how the groups were formed, who left, whether the groups were alike at the start.

ESSA Evidence Tiers

Tier Requires In this course
1  Strong At least one well-designed randomized trial Module 7
2  Moderate At least one well-designed quasi-experimental study Module 8
3  Promising At least one correlational study with statistical controls for selection bias Modules 1–6: regression
4  Demonstrates a rationale A logic model and a plan to evaluate Theory of action (Part 3)
Written into the Every Student Succeeds Act (2015). Several federal funding streams require Tiers 1–3.
A tier describes the design of the best available study. Not the size of the effect, the outcome, or the fit to your setting.

What a Rating Does Not Tell a Decision Maker

A rating certifies that the comparison in one study was fair. Four things it leaves to you.
Fit
Who was studied, where, with what dosage. Question 6 from Modules 7 and 8.
Size and precision
A rated study can have a small effect, or a wide interval.
Cost
Module 10.
The outcome
Early literacy tutoring: 46 studies, more than 25 different assessments. A fluency test favors a fluency program.
Novicoff & Loeb (2024)
A program with no rated study has not been shown to fail. It has not been studied with a design that can be rated.

Positive and Normative Claims

Positive: what is
What happened. What a program did. The claims research can make.
Normative: what ought to be
Which goods to weigh, and how. Policymaking is value-laden, and should be.
Module 11: Brighouse et al.; Furman
How evidence enters policy: in the short run it slots into agendas already set; in the medium and long run it can shift a debate.
School funding: for decades the correlational literature was read as “money does not matter much.” Quasi-experimental studies of finance reforms changed that reading because readers could see their causal warrant.Module 8: Jackson, Johnson & Persico (2016)

Theory of Action

Theory guides the questions asked, the constructs measured, and the mediators examined. Causal research is not atheoretical.
Same correlation, two theories
Human capital: schooling raises wages by raising productivity.
Signaling: schooling raises wages by certifying ability already there.
Different policies follow.
A theory of action for a coaching grant
Coaches and specialists → teacher practice → decoding and fluency → comprehension and test scores.

Each link can be measured. Each is a place implementation can break: the first year spent hiring; districts that could not find licensed coaches saw smaller effects.
Novicoff (2026), California LCRSG, 818 schools
A theory of action is also how to read a null result: which link failed?

Researcher Discretion

Garden of forking paths: sample, outcomes, covariates, measures, model. Enough choices produce p < .05 without intent to mislead.Gelman & Loken
29 teams, one dataset
Do referees give more red cards to players with darker skin?

20 of 29 teams found significant bias, with widely varying effect sizes. Discussion did not produce agreement.
Silberzahn & Uhlmann (2015)
73 teams, one hypothesis
Does immigration reduce support for social policy?

17% positive and significant  ·  25% negative and significant  ·  58% not significant.
Coded workflow choices explained about 5% of the variation.
Breznau et al. (2022)
File drawer: null results are harder to publish, so the published record over-represents significant ones.

Quasi-Experimental Studies Have More Forking Paths

A trial constrains
Treatment contrast fixed by design. Main analysis: compare means. Usually preregistered.
A quasi-experiment leaves open
Which comparison group, bandwidth, estimator, controls, years.
Nearly 22,000 test statistics
684 studies, 25 leading economics journals, 2015–2018. 21% experimental.

DiD and IV: too few results just below the significance threshold, too many just above. RCT and RD: no such pattern.
No better in selective journals, after revision, or over time.
Brodeur et al. (2020)
Statistical power, 31 journals
Median power: experiments 78%; quasi-experimental studies 7%.

Excess of significant findings: observational 19.1%; experimental 9.7%.
Askarov et al. (2023)

Preregistration

A pre-analysis plan fixes in advance
Confirmatory hypotheses  ·  outcomes  ·  sample  ·  design  ·  inference, including multiple comparisons.

Time-stamped before the data. Amendments visible.

Separates confirmatory from exploratory findings.
Rare for quasi-experiments
Registry of Efficacy and Effectiveness Studies: 606 designs; over 77% random assignment; 21% quasi-experimental; 9 regression discontinuity.

Economics and political science: 4% of pre-analysis plans used observational data.
Dee (2025); Ofosu & Posner (2023)
Dee’s proposal: adapted preregistration for quasi-experimental studies, with decision trees and amendments, and norms that publish confirmatory and exploratory results, labeled.
As a reader: look for a registration. Without one, treat many results as exploratory until replicated.

The Researcher’s Position

Passionate indifference
Rooting for an outcome is human. Much p-hacking comes from a belief that the effect must be there.

The truth about what works matters more than any single study. Null results teach.
The work
Highest quality you can. Humble, precise, thorough, well read on the topic.

Communicate clearly and in good faith. Causal claims are taken more seriously, so the quality has to carry that weight.
As a reader: notice when you want a study to be right.

Problem, Evidence, Decision

Kind of sentence Hypothetical brief on chronic absenteeism What to check
The problem “28 percent of the state’s students were chronically absent, up from 16 percent before the pandemic.” Descriptive: level, trend, comparison. For whom? How big?
What is known: association “Students who are chronically absent in kindergarten score lower in third grade.” Descriptive. Not a causal estimate.
What is known: causal “In a randomized trial, mailed reminders to families reduced absences by about 10 percent.” Does the brief say how the estimate was produced?
The recommendation “The state should fund mailed reminders statewide.” Does the evidence reach this far?
What is unknown Who was in the trial. Whether the effect lasts. Cost. Whether these families resemble the study’s. Named by the brief, or left to the reader?
