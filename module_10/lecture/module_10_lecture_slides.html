Budget Spending and Total Cost

Cost: the value of every resource a program uses, whoever pays for it.
In the program budget
New salaries and stipends
Purchased curricula and materials
Vendor and training fees
Also used by the program
Existing teachers’ and coaches’ time
Classrooms and other space
Hours spent in training
Volunteers and donated materials
Family and student time
Count the costs from the program as actually implemented, not from a generic or idealized description.
Hollands & Levin (2017)

The Ingredients Method

1  Identify
Describe the program model
Choose the perspective
List every ingredient, with its quantity and quality
2  Price
Find or estimate a price for each ingredient
Adjust prices to your context
3  Calculate
Total cost and cost per participant
Sensitivity analysis: how much the total depends on the assumptions
An effect estimate without the cost of producing it is incomplete evidence for a decision. A cost analysis reported alongside the effect makes a study usable by a district.Hollands & Levin (2017)
Three phases: Institute of Education Sciences (2020), Cost Analysis: A Starter Kit

The Program as Delivered

Program model
Who is served, and how many
How often, and for how long
Group size
Who delivers it, with what training
Perspective
Whose costs count: the school’s, the district’s, or every party’s, including families and partners.

A full cost analysis counts every party’s resources.
Module 9’s Kappan article: the Chapter One tutoring trial in a Florida district cost $375–$450 per student. Before comparing that figure with another program’s: does it include training, supervision, and space, or only the tutors?Novicoff & Loeb (2024)

Ingredient Categories

Personnel
Time of everyone involved: teachers, tutors, coordinators, administrators
Facilities
Rooms and space, including space the school already owns
Materials and equipment
Curricula, books, devices, software, assessments
Training
Trainer fees and the participants’ time
Other inputs
Transportation, food, family and student time

Pricing Ingredients

Market prices
Salary plus benefits, per hour of time used
Purchase prices for materials and equipment
National average prices
CostOut, a free IES-funded tool, includes a database of national average prices for education resources
Volunteer time and donated items
Priced at what the resource would cost to buy. Someone pays for it, even when the school does not.
Local adjustment
Adjust national prices to the district’s salaries and costs

Four Types of Cost Analysis

Type Question it answers Example
Cost-feasibility Can we afford to implement it as designed? Laptops for every student and teacher, with training
Cost-effectiveness Which program produces a given outcome at the lowest cost? Two reading programs, one reading outcome (Module 11)
Cost-benefit Do the benefits, valued in dollars, exceed the costs? A dropout prevention program
Cost-utility Which option do stakeholders value most, given its costs? Special education services, weighing several outcomes and preferences
Hollands & Levin (2017), adapted from Levin & McEwan (2001)
