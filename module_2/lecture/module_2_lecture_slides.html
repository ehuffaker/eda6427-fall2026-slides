Part 1
Binary Outcomes

From Scores to Decisions

A continuous score tells you where a child falls on a scale. A decision asks whether a child crosses a threshold.
  • Proficiency cut scores determine who receives intervention
  • Eligibility thresholds determine who receives services
  • Graduation is binary: the student completed the requirements or did not

We can still use regress to predict a yes/no outcome. The interpretation changes. The command does not.

Administrative decisions are usually threshold decisions. A cut score determines who gets intervention. An eligibility threshold determines who gets services. Graduation is binary.

The useful thing here is that you do not need a new command. You run regress exactly as before. What changes is how you read the coefficients.

Creating a Binary Outcome Variable

gen proficient = (x1rscalk1 >= 40) if !missing(x1rscalk1)

tab proficient

The variable proficient equals:

  • 1 if the child scored 40 or above
  • 0 if the child scored below 40

About 29 percent of fall kindergartners meet this threshold.

First we need a binary variable to predict. This line creates one: proficient equals 1 when the fall reading score is 40 or above, and 0 when it is below. The if not-missing condition keeps children with no score out of both categories rather than counting them as zeros. That detail matters, and it is a common error.

About 29 percent of children meet this threshold: 4,516 of the 15,669 with a reading score.

The Linear Probability Model

When we run regress with a 0/1 outcome, we get what is called a linear probability model. 
regress proficient x12sesl female fullday

Each coefficient is now the change in the probability of the outcome, in percentage points, for a one-unit increase in the predictor.

If the SES coefficient is 0.18: a one-standard-deviation increase in SES is associated with an 18 percentage-point increase in the probability of reading proficiency.

Same command, binary outcome. The result has a name: the linear probability model.

The interpretation is where the work is. A coefficient is no longer a change in score points. It is a change in the probability of the outcome, expressed in percentage points. A coefficient of 0.18 means 18 percentage points, not 18 percent and not 0.18 points.

Interpreting the Coefficients

SES = 0.18***
A one-standard-deviation increase in SES is associated with an 18 percentage-point increase in the probability of proficiency.

Female = 0.04***
Girls are about 4 percentage points more likely to be proficient than boys, holding SES and program type constant.

Full-day = 0.01 (not significant)
Full-day kindergarten is not distinguishable from zero once SES and gender are held constant.

Three coefficients, three sentences.

SES: 18 percentage points per standard deviation. That is a large association.

Female: about 4 percentage points, holding the other two constant.

Full-day: 1.5 percentage points with a p-value of 0.134. Grey box, because we cannot rule out zero. Note that we say it is not distinguishable from zero, not that it has no effect.

PlayPosit Question 1

A study finds that having a college-educated parent is associated with a 0.15 increase in the probability of kindergarten reading proficiency, estimated with a linear probability model. What does this mean?

  1. Children of college-educated parents score 15 points higher on reading
  2. Children of college-educated parents are 15 percentage points more likely to be proficient
  3. College-educated parents are 15% of the sample
  4. The coefficient is not statistically significant because it is less than 1

A study finds that having a college-educated parent is associated with a 0.15 increase in the probability of kindergarten reading proficiency, from a linear probability model. What does that mean?

The answer is B. Children of college-educated parents are 15 percentage points more likely to be proficient. A is the continuous-score reading, which would be right for an ordinary regression but not this one. C describes the sample composition, which the coefficient says nothing about. D is wrong because significance comes from the p-value, not the size of the number.

What LPMs Can and Cannot Do

Strengths
  • Uses the regress command you already know
  • Coefficients are easy to interpret, in percentage points
  • Widely used in education, economics, and policy research
Limitation
  • The model can predict probabilities below 0 or above 1
  • A very high-SES child might get a predicted probability of 1.05, which is not a probability
Logistic regression handles this by forcing predictions between 0 and 1. You will see it in published work. We do not estimate it in this course, and for most applied purposes the linear probability model is both acceptable and easier to read.

Strengths first: same command, interpretable coefficients, and it is genuinely common in published education research.

The limitation is real. Because the model fits a straight line, it can predict a probability above 1 or below 0 for children at the extremes. That is a defect of the model, not of the data.

Logistic regression solves it by constraining predictions to the 0 to 1 range. You will encounter it when you read research. We are not estimating it here. When you see it, know that it is answering the same question through a different functional form.

Part 2
Interaction Terms

A Result From Last Term

In the t-test module last term, we compared reading scores by kindergarten program type. Half-day children scored higher: 38.37 against 37.02, a difference of 1.35 points with p < .001.

That result was surprising. Does half-day kindergarten produce better readers?

We flagged at the time that this was unlikely to be causal, without the tools to look further.

Now we have them.

You have seen this comparison before. In the t-test module last term we compared fall reading scores for half-day and full-day kindergartners, and half-day children scored higher: 38.37 against 37.02, a gap of 1.35 points, highly significant with about 14,900 children.

That is a strange result if you read it as an effect of the program. We said at the time it was probably not causal. Now we can show why.

The Selection Problem

Full-day and half-day programs serve different populations. About 13.7 percent of half-day children are in poverty, against 27.5 percent of full-day children.

Higher-SES families are more likely to choose half-day programs, so the raw comparison confounds program type with family background.

Holding SES constant changes the picture, which is what the next few slides do.

Here is the reason. The two groups are not comparable. Poverty rates are twice as high in full-day programs: 27.5 percent against 13.7.

So the raw difference is partly a comparison of program types and partly a comparison of family circumstances. This is the selection problem you worked on last term, in a concrete case.

What If the Effect Is Not the Same for Everyone?

Holding SES constant, full-day kindergarten looks slightly positive on average. But does it do the same thing for every child?

Full-day programs might matter more for children from lower-SES households, where additional hours of instruction could partly substitute for resources available at home.

Or the association might be the same across the SES range, or absent entirely.

An interaction term lets us test whether the association between one variable and the outcome depends on the value of another variable.

An average effect can hide a great deal. A program that helps one group and does nothing for another can produce a small average that describes no actual child.

There is a plausible story here: extra hours of school might matter more for children with fewer academic resources at home. There is an equally plausible story where it makes no difference. The point is that we can test it rather than speculate.

The tool is the interaction term.

What Is an Interaction?

If the lines are parallel, there is no interaction. If the slopes differ, there is.

Two pictures. On the left, both groups have the same SES slope. Full-day children sit slightly higher everywhere, and the gap between the lines is constant. That is a main effect with no interaction.

On the right, the slopes differ. The gap is large at low SES and closes as SES rises. The full-day difference depends on where a child sits in the SES distribution. That is an interaction.

The whole test comes down to whether the lines are parallel.

The Interaction in an Equation

Without an interaction

Reading = constant + b₁(Full-Day) + b₂(SES) + error
The SES slope, b₂, is the same for full-day and half-day children.

With an interaction

Reading = constant + b₁(Full-Day) + b₂(SES) + b₃(Full-Day × SES) + error
  • b₂ is the SES slope when Full-Day = 0, that is, for half-day children
  • b₂ + b₃ is the SES slope when Full-Day = 1
  • b₃ is how much the SES slope changes for full-day children

The algebra is worth seeing once.

Without the interaction there is one SES slope, b two, and it applies to everyone.

Adding the product term gives b three. Now the SES slope is b two for half-day children, because the product is zero when full-day is zero. For full-day children the product equals SES, so the slope becomes b two plus b three.

b three is the difference between the two slopes. That is the number the model is testing.

Creating the Interaction Term

* Create the product of fullday and SES
gen fullday_ses = fullday * x12sesl

* Run the interaction model
regress x1rscalk1 fullday x12sesl fullday_ses

The interaction term is the product of the two variables.

When fullday is 0, the product is 0, so the interaction drops out and the SES coefficient alone gives the SES slope.

When fullday is 1, the product equals SES, so the interaction coefficient is added to the SES coefficient.

Stata can build this automatically with fullday##c.x12sesl. Creating the variable yourself keeps the mechanics visible, which is why we do it this way first.

Two lines. Generate the product, then put all three terms in the regression.

Work through what the product does. For a half-day child, fullday is zero, so the product is zero no matter what SES is, and the interaction contributes nothing. For a full-day child, fullday is one, so the product is just SES, and the interaction coefficient gets added to the SES slope.

There is a shortcut syntax with the double hash. Use it once you are comfortable. Building the variable by hand makes it obvious what is being estimated.

Now compare. Full-day moved from 0.28 to 0.44 and is now significant at the .05 level. SES moved from 4.78 to 5.51. And the interaction term is negative 0.88, significant at the .001 level.

Every one of those numbers changed because the previous model was averaging two different SES slopes into one. The next slide takes the three coefficients apart.

Reading the Interaction Output

fullday = 0.44*
The full-day difference for a child at average SES, where SES = 0. About half a reading point.

x12sesl = 5.51***
The SES slope for half-day children, where fullday = 0. Each standard deviation of SES predicts 5.5 more reading points.

fullday_ses = −0.88***
How much the SES slope changes for full-day children. The slope is 0.88 points less steep, so the SES gap is narrower in full-day programs.

Three coefficients, and each one answers a different question.

Full-day, 0.44, is not the full-day effect in general. It is the full-day difference for a child at average SES, because SES is standardized and zero is the mean.

The SES coefficient, 5.51, is not the SES slope in general either. It is the slope for half-day children.

The interaction, negative 0.88, is the difference between the two slopes. Because it is negative, the SES slope is flatter for full-day children, meaning the gap between low- and high-SES children is smaller in full-day programs.

Always compute the implied slopes. Never stop at the three raw coefficients.

PlayPosit Question 2

The SES slope for half-day children is 5.51. The interaction term is −0.88.

What is the SES slope for full-day children?

  1. 5.51 + 0.88 = 6.39
  2. 5.51 + (−0.88) = 4.63
  3. −0.88, because the interaction replaces the main effect
  4. 5.51 × −0.88 = −4.85

The SES slope for half-day children is 5.51 and the interaction is negative 0.88. What is the slope for full-day children?

The answer is B: 5.51 plus negative 0.88, which is 4.63. You add the interaction to the main effect. C is wrong because the interaction modifies the slope rather than replacing it, and D is wrong because these terms add, they do not multiply.

Visualizing the Interaction

The lines are not parallel. The SES slope is 5.51 in half-day programs and 4.63 in full-day programs.

This is the same model as a picture, and it is worth always drawing one.

Two lines, one per group, plotted across the SES range. They are not parallel, which is what the significant interaction told us.

At low SES, full-day children are predicted about 1.3 points higher. At average SES the difference is about 0.4. At high SES the lines have crossed and full-day children are slightly lower.

Writing Up an Interaction

After controlling for socioeconomic status, full-day kindergarten was associated with a modest increase in reading scores for the average child (b = 0.44, p = .035).

The relationship between SES and reading differed by program type. The SES slope was 5.51 points per standard deviation for half-day students and 4.63 for full-day students (interaction b = −0.88, p = .001).

This pattern suggests that the SES gap in early reading is narrower in full-day programs.

What a good interaction write-up includes:
  • Both the main effects and the interaction
  • The implied slopes for each group, computed for the reader
  • The direction stated in plain language

Here is what this looks like written up. Notice three things.

It reports the main effects and the interaction, not just the interaction. It gives the reader both implied slopes rather than making them do the arithmetic. And it states the direction in a sentence someone can act on.

Notice also what it does not say. It does not say full-day kindergarten narrows the gap. It says the gap is narrower in full-day programs. That distinction between describing a pattern and claiming an effect is the one to protect.

Part 3
Model Fit

How Well Does the Model Fit?

Every regression you have run printed these numbers at the top right. We have mostly read past them. They answer a different question from the coefficients.
  • A coefficient answers: how is this predictor related to the outcome?
  • A fit statistic answers: how much of the outcome does the whole model account for?

A model can contain a highly significant predictor and still account for very little of the variation. Significance and fit are separate questions, and reporting one without the other gives an incomplete picture.

Now the numbers in the top-right corner of every regression you have run.

The distinction to hold is that coefficients and fit statistics answer different questions. A coefficient tells you how one predictor relates to the outcome. A fit statistic tells you how much of the outcome the whole model accounts for.

These can point in different directions, and we will see a case in a moment where they do.

R-Squared and Adjusted R-Squared

R-squared is the share of variation in the outcome accounted for by the model. Our interaction model has R-squared of 0.1588, so it accounts for about 16 percent of the variation in reading scores.

R-squared has one property worth knowing: it can only rise when you add a predictor. Even a variable with nothing to contribute will nudge it upward.

Adjusted R-squared applies a penalty for each predictor. It rises only when a new variable earns its place, and it can fall.

A low R-squared does not mean the relationship is unimportant. It means other things also matter, which is nearly always true of educational outcomes.

R-squared is the share of variation the model accounts for. Sixteen percent for our interaction model, which leaves 84 percent to everything we did not measure.

The property that matters: R-squared can only go up when you add a variable. Add a column of random numbers and it will rise a little. So a rising R-squared by itself is not evidence that a model improved.

Adjusted R-squared charges a penalty per predictor. It goes up only if the new variable contributes more than that penalty, and it can go down. When someone asks whether adding variables improved the model, adjusted R-squared is the more honest number.

And say this plainly: a low R-squared is normal in education research. Human outcomes have many causes.

The F-Statistic

The F-statistic tests one question: taken together, do the predictors account for more variation than chance would produce?
Full-day only Add SES Add interaction
Observations 14,896 13,444 13,444
R-squared 0.0029 0.1581 0.1588
Adjusted R-squared 0.0028 0.1579 0.1586
F-statistic 42.60 1261.80 845.79
Root MSE 9.588 8.919 8.916
Between the second and third models, R-squared rises while F falls. Both are correct.

The F-statistic asks whether the predictors together account for more than chance would.

Now look across the row. F is 42.6 with full-day alone, jumps to 1,262 when SES enters, and then falls to 846 when the interaction is added, even though R-squared went up.

That happens because F depends on the number of predictors as well as the fit. Adding a term that contributes very little spreads the explained variation across more degrees of freedom, so F falls. A larger F does not mean a better model.

Look at adjusted R-squared in the same two columns: 0.1579 and 0.1586. Barely moved. The interaction is a real and significant finding about who the association differs for, and it adds almost nothing to how much of reading we can account for. Both statements are true at once, and a good write-up says both.

Root MSE is the typical prediction error in the outcome’s own units, about 8.9 reading points here. It is often the most concrete of the four.

PlayPosit Question 4

A researcher adds four control variables to a model. R-squared rises from 0.31 to 0.33, and adjusted R-squared falls from 0.30 to 0.29.

What is the most reasonable conclusion?

  1. The model improved, because R-squared went up
  2. The new variables added little beyond the penalty for including them
  3. The new variables are all statistically significant
  4. The model is now 33% accurate

R-squared rises from 0.31 to 0.33 while adjusted R-squared falls from 0.30 to 0.29. What is the most reasonable conclusion?

The answer is B. R-squared went up because R-squared always goes up when you add variables. Adjusted R-squared fell, which tells you the four variables did not contribute enough to cover the cost of including them.

A takes the rise in R-squared at face value, which is the error this question is about. C is wrong because fit statistics say nothing about individual coefficients. D is the “accuracy” misreading: R-squared is the share of variance accounted for, not a percentage of correct predictions.

What to Look For

1
What is the outcome? The title or header tells you what is being predicted.
2
What are the predictors? The leftmost column. Note which are added across models.
3
How big are the effects? Use the outcome’s scale to judge whether a coefficient is practically meaningful.
4
Which are significant? Check the stars against the footnote.
5
How well does the model fit? R-squared, and adjusted R-squared if reported. Does it improve across models?
6
Is there an interaction? A product term such as “Female × SES” tests whether one association depends on another.

Six questions, in order, for any regression table you meet.

What is the outcome. What are the predictors and how do they accumulate across columns. How big are the coefficients on the outcome’s own scale. Which are significant. How well does the model fit, and does the fit improve. And is there an interaction term hiding in the list.

That sixth one you could not have asked two weeks ago. Now a product term in a variable list should register immediately.

Walking Through the Table

Outcome: fall kindergarten math score.

Now the six questions applied.

The outcome is fall kindergarten math. Model 1 has SES alone at 5.96 with an R-squared of 0.193.

Model 2 adds demographics and SES drops to 5.74. That drop is informative: some of what Model 1 attributed to SES is shared with home language, gender, and disability status.

Model 3 adds the interaction, negative 0.73. Apply exactly what you did earlier: the SES slope is 6.10 for boys and 5.37 for girls.

PlayPosit Question 5

In the table, what happens to the SES coefficient between Model 1 and Model 2?

  1. It increases, meaning SES matters more once controls are added
  2. It decreases from 5.96 to 5.74, meaning some of the SES association was shared with the demographic variables

The answer is B. It falls from 5.96 to 5.74. When a coefficient moves after you add controls, the sensible reading is that the original estimate was carrying some of what those controls measure. It is a small movement here, but the reasoning is the same one you use for large ones.
