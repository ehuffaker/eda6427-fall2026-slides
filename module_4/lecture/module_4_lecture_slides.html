We have not talked about this in depth, but the ECLS-K dataset we have been working with since Module 1 is a large, carefully designed survey. The Department of Education did not field it by grabbing a random list of kindergartners or looking at a few schools. There was a multi-stage design. First they selected geographic areas across the country. Then they selected schools within those areas. Then they selected children within those schools. They also intentionally oversampled some groups, students from populations that are a small share of the overall population, to make sure the samples were large enough for meaningful analysis.

So every time we ran a regression, that survey design was underneath the data. We did not need to worry about it for the regression mechanics, but it matters for how you interpret results in practice: whether a survey is representative, and whether the designers took the effort to get a representative sample. Today is about understanding what is behind a dataset like this: who asked, who answered, and what conclusions we can draw.

Most district-level surveys fall at the bottom of this list. That is not a dealbreaker, but it limits what you can conclude.

There are four main ways a survey can select its participants, and they range from most rigorous to least.

Simple random sampling is the textbook ideal. Everyone in the population has an equal chance of being selected. It gives you the strongest basis for generalizing to the population, but in practice it is expensive and rarely used for large-scale studies. It is the sampling equivalent of a true experiment.

Stratified sampling divides the population into groups called strata, sometimes called blocks, and then samples within each group. NAEP, the National Assessment of Educational Progress, does this: it stratifies by race and ethnicity to ensure adequate subgroup representation in the final sample. The advantage is that you guarantee you will hear from key groups that a simple random sample might miss just by chance.

Cluster sampling selects the groups first, then surveys individuals within those groups. The ECLS-K works this way: sample schools, then sample children within the schools. It is efficient for populations that are naturally organized into groups, like students in schools. The trade-off is that people within the same cluster tend to be more similar to each other, which affects the precision of the estimates. If you ever hear about clustered standard errors, or nested or hierarchical models, this is the issue they are dealing with: students in the same school have related errors, and the standard errors have to account for that. We pick this up next module.

Convenience sampling is whoever shows up or responds. An online parent survey, a voluntary school climate questionnaire, an opt-in feedback form. Quick and cheap, but there is no random selection, so there is no firm statistical basis for generalizing to the population.

Most of the survey data you will encounter as education leaders or researchers comes from the bottom of this list. School climate surveys, parent satisfaction surveys, teacher feedback forms: these are almost always convenience samples. That does not mean they are not informative. It means you need to keep in mind what they can actually tell you, who actually responded, and that they cannot necessarily tell you what the whole population thinks.

PlayPosit Question 1

A state department of education wants to understand how principals feel about new testing requirements. They post a link to an online survey in their monthly newsletter. 800 principals respond (out of about 3,200 in the state). What type of sampling design is this?

  1. Simple random sample
  2. Stratified sample
  3. Cluster sample

A state department of education wants to understand how principals across the state feel about new testing requirements. They post a link to an online survey in their monthly newsletter. 800 principals respond, out of about 3,200 in the state. What type of sampling design is this?

The answer is D, a convenience sample. Even though the response count is large, there was no random selection. The survey went out to everyone, but only those who happened to read the newsletter and chose to click responded. The 800 who responded may feel very differently about testing requirements than the 2,400 who did not. The design tells us what these 800 principals think. It cannot tell us what principals statewide think.

Even well-designed probability samples face this fundamental challenge: a lot of people do not respond to surveys, and the people who do not respond are systematically different from those who do. This is nonresponse bias, and it is related conceptually to the selection bias we have already talked about. Response rates have been declining for decades, and the decline affects all survey research, including in education.

Think about the parent satisfaction survey from the opening question. Who fills it out? Typically parents who are engaged with the school, have time, speak the language the survey is in, and have access to whatever platform it is on. Who does not? Parents working multiple jobs, navigating a language barrier, or disengaged from the school entirely. So when we say “92% of parents are satisfied,” what that may really mean is that 92% of the parents who are already most engaged are satisfied. That is a different conclusion.

Who Responds Matters More Than How Many

What matters most: are the people who responded different from those who didn’t on what you’re measuring?
Illustrative values.

Response rates get a lot of attention, but they are not the whole story in judging the quality of survey data.

Consider two scenarios. Scenario A is an 80% response rate, but the respondents are 90% white in a school that is 60% white. High response rate, but the sample does not look like the population. Scenario B is a school with only a 35% response rate, but the respondent demographics closely match the school. Lower response rate, but more representative.

Which gives more trustworthy results? Typically Scenario B. Representativeness can be just as important as the response rate, if not more. The question is not just how many people responded, but to what extent the people who responded reflect the people who did not, on the things we are measuring.

Three examples where this matters in education. Parent surveys: engaged, higher-SES parents tend to be overrepresented. Teacher surveys: teachers experiencing burnout may be less likely to complete a workplace climate survey. Student surveys: students who are chronically absent may miss the survey day, and if absenteeism is correlated with the outcomes we are measuring, the data systematically exclude the students we may need to hear from most.

The answer is C. The response rate alone does not tell you whether the findings are trustworthy. Who is missing is the real question.

Part 3
Weights and Missing Data

What Survey Weights Do

What weights do
Adjust for oversampling and nonresponse so the sample looks more like the population. Stata has tools for this (svyset, [pw=]); we won’t use them, but you should know they exist.
Key limitation
Weights can only adjust for things you can measure. If frustrated parents don’t respond and you have no data on frustration, no weight can fix that.
Illustrative values.

When certain groups are over- or under-represented in a survey, one common fix is survey weights. Weights are statistical adjustments that make the sample look more like the population, and they are a common feature of survey research.

The basic idea: if low-income families were oversampled so you would have enough of them to analyze, weights scale them back to their true population share when you calculate estimates. If certain demographic groups had lower response rates, you weight to adjust their share upward.

The ECLS-K includes weight variables for exactly this reason. We have not been using them; we have been running unweighted regressions, which is fine for learning regression. A researcher publishing from the ECLS-K would want to account for the weights. Stata has several tools for this, such as the svyset command and probability weights. Weights come up with administrative data too: in the Florida Department of Education data, some districts are much larger than others, and in some cases you may want to weight by the number of students so that larger districts count more heavily.

We are not going to use weights in this course, although if you want to use them in your final project, you may. The point is awareness: when someone presents survey results and generalizes to a population, it is worth asking whether they used appropriate weights.

The key limitation is that weights can only adjust for things we can measure. If parents who are frustrated with the school do not respond to a climate survey, and we have no way to identify which parents are frustrated, we cannot weight our way out of that bias.

A final key concept worth naming this week: missing data. This is somewhat different from nonresponse, where someone does not take the survey at all. Missing data is when someone skips certain questions or individual records are incomplete. There are three main types. The names sound technical, but the idea is simple: is the reason the data are missing related to what is missing?

MCAR is missing completely at random. The missingness has nothing to do with the content of the questions or the respondent. A computer glitch that randomly deletes 5% of records is the classic example. The estimates are not biased; you just have a smaller sample. It is rare in practice.

MAR is missing at random. The missingness is related to something we can see in the data. For example, boys are more likely to skip reading-attitudes questions, but given gender, their answers would look similar to everyone else’s. Advanced methods can handle this.

MNAR, missing not at random, is the most concerning. The missingness is related to the missing value itself. Students with the lowest test scores are the ones most likely to be absent on testing day. High-income families are the least likely to report income. This is a real problem: it biases our estimates, and we cannot fully fix it with the data we have. So when you see missing responses to particular questions, think carefully about why those data might be missing, and whether you have a theory for what could be driving it.

A practical connection: remember how your number of observations changed between regressions when we added more controls? That is Stata doing listwise deletion, dropping any observation that is missing any variable included in the regression. Different variables have different missingness patterns, so different models use different subsets of the data. Two questions to ask whenever you see missing data: how much is missing, and is there a pattern?

A district collects end-of-year reading scores for all 3rd graders. Students who were absent on testing day, about 12% of enrollment, have no scores. Chronically absent students are disproportionately represented in this group. Which type of missing data is this?

The answer is C, missing not at random. The students who are missing are likely to have lower reading scores, because chronic absenteeism is associated with lower academic achievement. The missingness is related to the value that would have been observed. That means the district’s average reading score is probably higher than the true average, because the lowest-performing students are systematically excluded.

Everything so far has been about evaluating survey evidence that other people give you. But at some point in your career you will be on the other side: designing, administering, or commissioning a survey for a school, a district, or another organization. School climate surveys, parent satisfaction surveys, teacher needs assessments, program evaluations, strategic planning input. These are core tools of educational leadership, and of leadership in general.

The rest of today is practical guidance for when you are in that role. It may also help those of you considering survey data for your dissertation research. You can take full courses in survey design, and I would encourage that if you plan to build a survey into a dissertation. This is a starting point: the key principles for collecting useful data from surveys.

Start with the Decision

1
What decision will this survey inform?
Not “we should survey parents”: name the specific decision, or the research question.
↓
2
Who needs to be heard for that decision?
If it affects all families, you need a cross-section, not just PTA regulars.
↓
3
How will you reach them?
Email, paper, phone, text? Multiple modes, multiple languages.
If you can’t name the decision, don’t field the survey.

Start with the decision you will be making, or if this is a research survey, the research question you want to answer. A common problem is fielding a survey without a clear purpose. “We should survey parents about school climate and see what we find” is not a clear enough purpose. “We need to decide whether to restructure our after-school programming, and parent input will inform that decision” is a specific purpose.

Starting with the decision leads to the next question: who needs to be heard for that decision to be made, or who needs to be in the sample to answer the research question? If the decision or the intervention affects all families, you need input from a representative cross-section, not just the parents who show up to PTA meetings, and not just the students who volunteer for an after-school program.

Then: how do you reach them? Email works for some families but not others. A paper survey sent home might reach families you miss online. Offering the survey in multiple languages matters, and text can expand your reach. The mode of administration shapes who responds. Design backward from the decision when creating the instrument.

Getting People to Respond

1
Keep it short. Fewer questions, higher completion. Cut anything that doesn’t inform the decision.
2
Make it easy. Multiple modes, multiple languages, an accessible format.
3
Send reminders. One follow-up substantially boosts response; two or three, more so.
4
Explain the “why.” People respond when they believe their input matters and know how results will be used.
5
Time it well. Avoid testing weeks, the end of a term, and competing surveys.
6
Check who’s missing. Compare respondent demographics to your population before closing; follow up where groups are underrepresented.

Six practical strategies that meaningfully improve the quality of your data.

One: keep it short. Fewer questions leads to higher completion. Anything that does not directly inform the research question or the decision you identified, exclude.

Two: make it easy. Multiple modes, online plus paper. Multiple languages. An accessible format that is easy to navigate.

Three: send reminders. One follow-up can substantially increase response rates. Two or three, even more so.

Four: the framing. Explain why. People respond more when they believe their input matters. Tell them what the survey is for and how the results will be used.

Five: time it well. Avoid periods of survey fatigue: the end of a term, testing weeks, right before a holiday, or a time when several other surveys are being fielded.

Six, and this one relates most directly to how useful the data will be: check who is missing before you close the survey. Compare the respondents to the population you need to hear from for the decision or the research question. If certain groups are underrepresented, you may still have time to do a targeted follow-up.

Question Design Pitfalls

Watch out for

Leading
“Do you agree that our school provides excellent instruction?”
“How would you rate the quality of instruction at your school?”

“How satisfied are you with school safety and communication?”

A few common challenges in survey questions, whether you are writing them yourself or reviewing ones other people have created.

Leading questions push respondents toward a particular answer. “Do you agree that our school provides excellent instruction?” is leading. A more neutral version: “How would you rate the quality of instruction at your school?”

Double-barreled questions ask two things at once. “How satisfied are you with school safety and communication?” A parent could be happy with safety but unhappy with communication, and they do not know what to answer. Ask about separate concepts separately.

Social desirability bias is a big challenge for both practical and academic surveys. People give the answer they think is expected. If you ask how much someone values their child’s education, everyone says a lot. It is better to ask about specific behaviors: how often do you read with your child at home?

These are things to review before a survey goes out. Having a few other people try to respond to the draft is a low-lift way to improve clarity and quality before you send it.

PlayPosit Question 4

You’re a principal planning a survey to understand why some families don’t attend parent-teacher conferences. Which approach is most likely to produce useful, representative data?

  1. Post a link on the school website and in the weekly email newsletter
  2. Hand out a paper survey at the next parent-teacher conference

You are a principal planning a survey to understand why some families do not attend parent-teacher conferences. Which approach is most likely to produce useful, representative data?

The answer is C: send a short survey home in student backpacks in multiple languages, with a phone option and two follow-up reminders. This reaches families regardless of internet access, accounts for language barriers, and uses reminders to improve response rates.

Notice why option B is particularly problematic: handing out surveys at parent-teacher conferences would only reach parents who already attend. You would be surveying the wrong population entirely. You want to hear from the families who do not come, not the ones who do.

A brief review of regression using survey data. Many of the variables we have been working with in these models came from surveys. Does that matter for the interpretation we have been doing? Here is an example of a regression predicting reading scores using survey data. One of the predictors is a school climate score reported by parents on a one-to-five scale. Those one-to-five scales that run from strongly disagree to strongly agree are called Likert scales. The coefficient is 2.14, so the interpretation is the same as always: a one-point increase on the climate scale is associated with 2.14 points higher reading.

Three things to watch for when a predictor comes from a survey. First, know what a one-unit increase means. On a five-point Likert scale it is the difference between agree and strongly agree, a specific, bounded shift, not an open-ended increase. Second, self-reported measures can carry social desirability bias into the model. If parents over-report satisfaction, that measurement error is now loaded onto the predictor. Third, missing survey items mean Stata drops those cases. If the climate question has more missing values than the other variables, your sample becomes smaller than you expect, and the sample composition shifts.

None of this means you cannot use survey data in regression. They are things to note when you interpret your results, and when you read other people’s papers, which typically rely on survey data.

Part 6
Evaluating Survey Evidence

Can You Trust This Survey?

1
Who was sampled? Probability design or convenience? Census or sample?
2
Who responded? Response rate? Do respondent demographics match the population?
3
How were the questions asked? Leading questions, double-barreled items, social desirability?
4
What population can this speak to? Does the claim match what the design actually supports?
Last module: five questions for test-score data. This module: four questions for survey data. Two checklists for two types of evidence.

Here is the takeaway for approaching survey data and survey research. When someone presents survey results, at a board meeting, in a research paper, or in a policy report, a few questions tell you how much weight to give the findings.

One: who was sampled? Was this a probability design or a convenience sample? A census of the full population, or a subsample?

Two: who responded? What was the response rate? Do the demographics of respondents match the population that was expected?

Three: how were the questions asked? Were there leading questions, double-barreled items, or social desirability concerns? The instruments used to collect survey data are almost always available, in the appendices of a paper or through the agency that ran the survey, such as NCES. They usually publish the full instrument, so you can review the questions yourself.

Four: what population can this speak to? Does the claim match what the design actually supports? This is the same kind of question we ask about claims in any quantitative research.

So you now have a checklist for general quantitative research, a checklist for research using test scores, and now one for survey data.
