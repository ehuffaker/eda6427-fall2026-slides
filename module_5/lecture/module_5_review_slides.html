Every Score Has Some Error

Observed score  =  True score  +  Error
  • Take the same test twice and your score moves, even with no new learning.
  • Reliability is how consistent a measure is. Standardized tests report it in their technical documents.
  • This connects back to test scores in Module 3.

Start with measurement error. Any score we record is really two things added together: the true score, meaning what the student actually knows, and some error. If you took the same test twice, your score would likely move a little, even if you learned nothing new in between. Maybe you were tired, or a few questions happened to suit you.

Reliability is how consistent a measure is. A highly reliable test gives about the same score each time. Standardized tests report their reliability in their technical documents, which connects back to what we covered on test scores in Module 3.

What Error Does to a Relationship

Error in a predictor makes the relationship look weaker than it is.

Why does this matter for regression? These three panels use simulated data. The true relationship is the same in all three; the only difference is how much error is in the predictor. On the left there is a lot of error, and the line is nearly flat. On the right there is very little error, and the line is steep.

So error in a predictor makes a relationship look weaker than it really is. The noise hides part of the signal.

What to Do About It

  • Check the reliability of the measures you use.
  • Prefer standardized, validated tests.
  • A noisy measure (a school-made or subjectively graded assessment) does less harm as an outcome than as a predictor.
Error in an outcome
Mostly makes estimates less precise
Error in a predictor
Shrinks its coefficient toward zero

A few practical takeaways. Check the reliability of the measures you use. When you can, choose standardized, validated tests. And if you have a noisier measure, like a school-made assessment or something graded subjectively, it does less harm as an outcome than as a predictor. Error in an outcome mostly makes estimates less precise, while error in a predictor shrinks its coefficient.

R-squared vs. Adjusted R-squared

From the first data assignment: adding school location to the model.
Model 2 Model 3 (adds school location)
R-squared 0.6573 0.6574
Adjusted R-squared 0.6572 0.6572
  • R-squared never goes down when you add predictors.
  • Adjusted R-squared subtracts a penalty for each one. It stayed flat, so school location added almost nothing.
  • To judge whether new predictors improved a model, look at adjusted R-squared.

Next, R-squared and adjusted R-squared. These numbers are from Models 2 and 3 in the first data assignment. Going from Model 2 to Model 3, we added school location, and R-squared went from 0.6573 to 0.6574.

R-squared never goes down when you add predictors, even ones that add nothing. Adjusted R-squared subtracts a small penalty for each predictor you add. Here it stayed flat at 0.6572, which tells us school location added almost nothing to how well the model explains reading scores. So when you want to know whether adding predictors improved a model, look at adjusted R-squared.

Significant but Small

Same model, school location compared with city schools.
Difference in reading points Statistically significant?
Town schools +1.04 Yes (p < .001)
Rural schools +1.11 Yes (p < .001)
R-squared 0.6573 → 0.6574
  • With about 12,000 children, even small differences are statistically significant.
  • Significance: the difference is unlikely to be zero. Explanatory power: how much it accounts for in the outcome.

The same model makes the last point. After accounting for fall reading and family background, children in town schools scored about 1.04 points higher than children in city schools, and children in rural schools about 1.11 points higher. Both differences are statistically significant. Yet R-squared barely moved.

How can both be true? With about 12,000 children, even small differences can be estimated precisely, so they come out significant. Statistical significance tells you a difference is unlikely to be zero. Explanatory power tells you how much that difference accounts for in the outcome. For a school or district leader deciding what to act on, a one-point difference that explains almost none of the variation is not much to go on.
