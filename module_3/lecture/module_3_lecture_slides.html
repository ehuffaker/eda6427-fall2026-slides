Zooming out. All semester we have been running regressions with test scores as the outcome variable: reading scores in ECLS-K, mean reading scores in the Florida district data. We have predicted them with SES, poverty, gender, full-day kindergarten, language status.

But we have never stopped to ask: what kind of score is this? Does the type of score change the story? The answer is yes, and that is what today is about.

Three things today. First, the proficiency rate: the metric that dominates education policy, and why it can be deeply misleading. Second, the alternatives: scaled scores and standardized scores, and how the same regression tells different stories depending on which score you use. Third, measuring growth: value-added measures and two practical approaches to modeling whether students are improving over time.

Why We Use Test Scores

Why They’re Useful
  • Predict outcomes we care about (graduation, college, earnings)
  • Gauge whether learning is occurring (common, comparable signal)
  • Inform instructional decisions (intervention, enrichment, skill gaps)
  • Enable accountability and evaluation (school performance, program effects)
Limitations
  • Capture only part of what schools do (not creativity, motivation, citizenship)
  • A snapshot, not a full picture (one test, one day, one domain)
  • High stakes distort behavior (teaching to the test, narrowed curriculum)
  • Inequality in testing conditions (resources, test prep access, bias)
Test scores are valuable. Understanding their limitations makes them more so.

Before we talk about what can go wrong with test scores, what are they good for, and where are their limits?

On the useful side: test scores predict long-run outcomes we care about. They correlate with high school graduation, college enrollment, and earnings. They give us a common, comparable signal for whether learning is occurring; without measurement, we are guessing. They inform instructional decisions: identifying which students need intervention, which are ready for enrichment. And they enable accountability: holding schools accountable, evaluating programs, and allocating resources all require some form of measurement.

But test scores also have real limitations. They capture only a slice of what schools do: not creativity, not motivation, not civic engagement. A test score is a snapshot: one test, one day, one domain. When you attach high stakes to that number, it distorts behavior: schools narrow the curriculum, teachers teach to the test, and the score starts measuring test preparation rather than broad learning. And there are inequalities in testing conditions themselves: differences in resources, test prep access, and potential test bias.

Both sides are true at the same time. Test scores are valuable, and understanding their limitations makes them more so.

Part 1
Proficiency Rates

Proficiency Rates

Proficiency rate: the percentage of students who score at or above a predetermined cut score on a standardized test.
Example: “65% of 4th graders scored proficient or above in reading.”

1
Accountability — school grades, state report cards, federal reporting under ESSA
2
Identifying students — who needs intervention? Who’s on track? Who qualifies for services?
3
Comparing subgroups — achievement gaps by race, income, disability, English learner status
Simple and intuitive: but simple doesn’t mean accurate.

Start with the number everyone knows: the proficiency rate. A proficiency rate is the percentage of students who score at or above a predetermined cut score on a standardized test. “65% of our 4th graders are proficient in reading” is a proficiency rate.

It is the dominant metric in education, and as a school leader you will encounter it constantly. Three main uses. First, accountability. When your state assigns a school grade, proficiency rates are a major input. Federal reporting under ESSA requires states to report proficiency. When a newspaper runs a headline about school performance, it is almost always proficiency rates.

Second, identifying students. Who needs intervention? Who is on track? Who qualifies for supplemental services? The answer often comes down to whether they are above or below the threshold.

Third, comparing subgroups. Achievement gap reporting by race, income, disability status, or English learner status is almost always done with proficiency rates.

Proficiency rates are simple and intuitive, and that is a real strength. But simple does not always mean accurate. Here is what happens under the surface.

The Funhouse Mirror

Viewing school performance through proficiency rates is like looking in a funhouse mirror. The answers about growth and equity are “at best distorted and at worst just wrong.”
— Andrew Ho, Harvard Graduate School of Education
1
Arbitrary cut scores — the threshold is a political choice, not a scientific one
2

3
Misreading achievement gaps — gap trends depend on where groups sit relative to the threshold

Andrew Ho is a measurement expert at Harvard’s Graduate School of Education, and his critique is the required reading this week. He describes proficiency rates as viewing school performance through a funhouse mirror. The image is recognizable and related to reality, but it is distorted, and sometimes the distortion is severe enough to mislead.

In his words, the answers proficiency rates give about growth and equity are “at best distorted and at worst just wrong.”

He identifies three specific problems, and we walk through each one.

Problem one: the cut score is arbitrary.

This is a distribution of reading scores: the same students, the same test. The only thing that changes between the two panels is where we draw the proficiency line. With the lower threshold, 62% of students are proficient. Move it up, and only 31% are. Same kids, same test, different headline.

The process for setting cut scores is what Ho calls “an overwrought, judgmental, and ultimately political process.” Each state sets its own standards, which is why proficiency rates are not comparable across states.

Mississippi is the standard example: its state test reported 84% of fourth graders proficient or above in reading while the national NAEP assessment put the figure at 18%. Same children. The difference is entirely about where each test drew the line.

So when someone says “70% of our students are proficient,” the first question to ask is: proficient by whose definition?

Problem 2: Distorted Growth

When students cluster near the threshold, small gains produce large proficiency swings. The reverse is also true.

Problem two: proficiency rates distort the appearance of growth.

Here we have a distribution of scores in Year 1 and Year 2. There has been a small, real improvement: the curve shifted right by about 2 points. Modest. But the proficiency rate jumped 11 percentage points, from 44% to 56%. That looks like a dramatic improvement. It is not. The actual learning gain was small.

Why does this happen? The cut score sits near the peak of the distribution, where students are most densely clustered. A tiny nudge in scores pushes a large number of students across the line.

Now flip it around. A school where most students are well above or well below the threshold could make real, meaningful improvements and see zero change in proficiency rates, because none of those students crossed the line. The metric is blind to any movement that does not cross the threshold.

This is why you cannot use proficiency rates to measure growth. They are a snapshot with a built-in distortion.

Problem 3: Misreading Achievement Gaps

Both groups improved equally on the actual test. Proficiency rates tell a different story.

Problem three, and this one is the most counterintuitive: proficiency rates can make achievement gaps look like they are narrowing when they are not, or widening when they are not.

Two groups, A and B. Both improved by the same amount on the actual test: their distributions shifted right by three points each.

But Group A’s proficiency rate went from 16% to 28%, while Group B’s went from 50% to 67%. Group B’s rate jumped about four percentage points more, and the gap between the two groups widened from 34 to 38 percentage points, even though both groups improved equally.

Why? Group B was centered right at the cut score, where students are most densely clustered, so a small shift moved more of them across the line. Group A sat further from the threshold, with fewer students near the line.

If you track disparities across student groups using only proficiency rates, you might celebrate progress that is not real or flag problems that do not exist. The gaps you see in proficiency rates are partly a statistical artifact of where each group sits relative to the cut score.

The neighboring district at 82% could be making similar actual gains and showing no movement, because few of its students are near the threshold.

If proficiency rates are the funhouse mirror, what is the real mirror?

The first alternative is the one you have been using all semester: scaled scores. A scaled score is the continuous number a student earns on the test. On the ECLS-K reading assessment, that is roughly a 20-to-70 scale. In the Florida district data, it was the mean reading scale score.

Instead of compressing every student into proficient or not proficient, scaled scores preserve the full distribution. You can see improvement at the bottom. You can see whether high-performing students are being challenged. No information is thrown away. When you regress a mean reading score on SES, the regression uses every point of variation in that scale; it does not care about a threshold.

One more thing worth knowing: you do not have to limit yourself to the overall summative score. Many assessments report sub-scores by content area, and some provide item-level data. For administrators this can be more actionable than a single number: if overall reading is flat but the comprehension sub-score is declining, that tells you where to focus instructional support.

The limitation of scaled scores is that they are test-specific. A score of 41 on the ECLS-K reading assessment means nothing on a state math test. You cannot compare scores across different tests, or even across years if the scale changes. That is where standardized scores come in.

You have already seen standardized scores: z-scores. You transform any score by subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation. The result has a mean of zero and a standard deviation of one.

Why is that useful? Because now you can compare across tests. A student half a standard deviation above the mean in reading is performing at a similar relative level as one half a standard deviation above the mean in math, even though the raw scales are completely different.

This is also where effect sizes come from. When a study reports that a program “improved reading by 0.15 standard deviations,” that is a standardized-score comparison. The Kraft benchmarks — small below 0.05, medium from 0.05 to 0.20, large above 0.20 — only make sense because the scores are standardized.

For administrators, standardized scores are the common language of education research. If you are reading a study or comparing results across programs, this is the metric that makes apples-to-apples comparison possible.

Part 3
Value-Added and Growth

What Is Value-Added?

This is regression. The residual — the gap between predicted and actual — is the value added.

Now our third section. Proficiency rates, scaled scores, and standardized scores all describe how students performed at one point in time. They are snapshots. But leaders care about growth: is this school actually helping students learn more than they would have otherwise?

Value-added measures try to answer that question, and the idea builds directly on regression.

Start with what you would predict a student will score based on their characteristics: demographics, prior achievement, maybe school and neighborhood factors. That is your regression prediction. Then look at what the student actually scored. The difference between predicted and actual is the value added.

If a school’s students consistently score higher than predicted, that school has positive value-added. If they consistently score below predicted, negative value-added.

This is not a new statistical technique. It is the regression you already know. The residual, the gap between the line and the dot, is what we could not explain with our variables. Value-added says: maybe the school explains some of that residual.

VAM: The Promise and the Problems

The Promise
  • Fairer to high-poverty schools (adjusts for student characteristics)
  • Captures growth, not just levels
  • Identifies unusually effective schools and teachers
  • Uses the full score distribution
The Problems
  • Estimates are volatile (ratings swing year to year)
  • Results depend on model choices (which controls are included)
  • Small classes amplify noise
  • Score inflation (Koretz): “gains 3–6× larger than real learning improvements”
You don’t need to build a VAM. You need to know the limitations when someone else does.

Value-added has real appeal. It adjusts for student characteristics, so it is fairer to high-poverty schools. It captures growth rather than just levels. It uses the full score distribution. And it can help identify schools or teachers who are unusually effective, or who need more support.

But there are serious problems. First, the estimates are volatile. A teacher’s value-added rating can swing substantially from one year to the next with no change in their actual teaching, and small class sizes amplify the noise.

Second, results depend heavily on which variables are in the model. Different control variables can produce different rankings. As you have seen, adding or removing a predictor changes the other coefficients.

Third, and this is Daniel Koretz’s central warning, linked as an optional reading this week: when test scores become high-stakes targets, the scores become less trustworthy. Schools and teachers face enormous pressure to raise scores, and the result is what Koretz calls score inflation: gains on high-stakes tests that are, in his estimation, three to six times as large as real gains in learning.

You do not need to build a value-added model. But as a leader, you need to understand what someone is claiming when they say they have measured school effectiveness or teacher value-added, and you need to know the limitations.

Approach 1: Controlling for Baseline Achievement

Reading (Spring)  =  β₀  +  β₁(SES)  +  β₂(Reading, Fall)  +  ε
↑  Lagged achievement — the baseline score
What this does:
  • The same regress command you’ve been running — one more variable on the right-hand side
  • Accounts for where each student started — coefficients now represent associations beyond baseline
  • R² jumps dramatically, because prior achievement is a very strong predictor
  • Adding a strong predictor reshapes what the other coefficients mean
If your data include a baseline score, seriously consider using it.

Two practical approaches to modeling growth, and both are things you can implement yourself.

The first: if you have access to a prior test score, add it as a control variable in your regression. That is the whole move. The same regress command, with one more variable on the right-hand side.

The equation should look familiar. Spring reading score as the outcome, predicted by SES, and now also by the fall reading score: the baseline. That baseline variable is called lagged achievement. It captures where the student started.

Why does this matter? Because controlling for where students started lets you get much closer to measuring growth. Once the baseline is in the model, the coefficients on your other variables represent their relationship with achievement beyond the starting point, which is more informative than their correlation with overall levels. It also improves the precision of the model, because baseline scores explain a large share of the variance.

Most of the time, if you have access to a prior test or pre-test, you will want to include it. It is one of the most powerful things you can do to improve a model in education research.

Look at the difference. On the left, the model without a baseline: predicting spring reading from SES, gender, and poverty. The R-squared is 0.17 and the SES coefficient is 4.8.

On the right, we add the fall reading score. The R-squared jumps to 0.62: the baseline score alone explains about 45 percentage points of additional variation. That is a large improvement in model fit, and it is representative of the jumps you see in actual data.

And look at what happened to SES. It dropped from 4.8 to 1.9. Some of what looked like an SES association was baseline achievement: higher-SES students started with higher scores, and that was driving part of the apparent SES-achievement relationship.

The logic is one you have seen before: when you add a strong predictor, it reshapes what the other coefficients mean. The baseline score is usually the strongest predictor you can add.

This is the simplest and most practical version of a growth model. Anyone with two time points of data can do it.

Approach 2: Growth as the Outcome

Growth (gain score)  =  Spring Score  −  Fall Score
Then:   Growth  =  β₀  +  β₁(SES)  +  β₂(Female)  +  ε

Advantages
  • Directly models who grew more
  • Intuitive for stakeholders
  • Easy to explain in a report
Tradeoffs
  • Growth scores are noisier
  • Regression to the mean: low scorers tend to “grow” more
This difference score is called a gain score. Both approaches are legitimate: researchers prefer controlling for baseline; gain scores are common in practice.

[CORRECTION vs. recording] Content addition: the slide now names the term “gain score” explicitly, which the syllabus promises for this module. The recording describes the same approach as “growth as the outcome” without using the term.

The second approach is even more intuitive. Instead of controlling for the baseline, make the change itself the outcome. Subtract the fall score from the spring score, and that difference, the gain score, becomes your Y variable.

Now your regression directly models who grew more. Did students with higher SES grow faster? Did students in the program grow more than students not in the program? The coefficients answer those questions directly.

The advantage is clarity. “Who grew more” is a straightforward question for stakeholders, and it is easy to explain in a meeting or a report.

The tradeoff: gain scores are noisier. Both the pre-test and the post-test have measurement error, and subtracting one from the other can amplify that noise. There is also a statistical pattern called regression to the mean, which we cover in a few slides: students who score very low on the pre-test tend to score higher on the post-test, and vice versa, even without any real change.

Both approaches are legitimate, and researchers debate which is better. Controlling for the baseline is generally preferred in the research literature, but gain scores are common in practice.

This table lays out the comparison side by side: what you model, how you interpret it, the strengths, the limitations, and when each approach makes sense.

The key point: they answer slightly different questions. Controlling for the baseline asks who scored higher than expected given where they started. Growth as the outcome asks who grew the most. Both are valuable, and the right choice depends on your research question.

If you have two time points and are not sure which to use, controlling for the baseline is the safer default: better precision, and more widely accepted in research. If growth is explicitly your question and your audience needs an intuitive story, the gain-score approach works too.

Either way, both of these are just regression. You already have the skills. The only thing that changes is what goes on the left-hand side of the equation, and what goes on the right.

Part 4
Other Score Issues

Floor and Ceiling Effects

If a test is too easy or too hard for the population taking it, scores cluster at the boundary. Real differences are hidden, and growth cannot be accurately measured. Watch for this in district benchmark assessments and end-of-course exams.

Three more things to know about test scores. Each comes up regularly, and each can mislead you if you are not watching for it.

First: floor and ceiling effects. If a test is too hard for the students taking it, scores pile up at the bottom: a floor effect. If it is too easy, scores pile up at the top: a ceiling effect. Either way, the test cannot distinguish between students at the extremes.

This matters for growth measurement. If your students are already near the top of the scale, you cannot measure further improvement, because the test has no room. If they are at the bottom, you cannot tell who is really struggling versus who just hit the minimum.

Watch for this with district benchmark assessments and end-of-course exams. If the score distribution looks like it is being cut off at one end, the test may not be a good fit for your population.

Regression to the Mean

Why it matters

A low-performing school shows “improvement” after a new program is introduced.

Was it the program? Or were those extreme scores partly random, and due to bounce back?

Controlling for baseline achievement (Approach 1) helps: it accounts for where students started.

Every score has some randomness. Extreme scores tend to move toward the average on retest.

Second: regression to the mean. This one is subtle but important, especially for program evaluation.

Every test score has some randomness in it. A student might score unusually low on one test because they were tired, guessed poorly, or had a bad day. On the next test, that randomness is unlikely to repeat, so their score bounces back toward the average.

Now imagine a school that scored unusually low last year. They implement a new reading program. This year, scores go up. Was it the program? Maybe. But some of that apparent improvement is regression to the mean: the extreme score was partly random, and it was due to bounce back regardless.

This is exactly why controlling for baseline achievement helps. It accounts for where students started, so what is left over is more likely to reflect real change rather than statistical bounce.

The takeaway: be cautious about attributing improvement to an intervention when the starting point was extreme. Some of that growth would have happened anyway.

Campbell’s Law and Test Validity

“The more any quantitative social indicator is used for social decision-making, the more subject it will be to corruption pressures and the more apt it will be to distort and corrupt the social processes it is intended to monitor.”
— Donald Campbell (1979)
Validity

Third: Campbell’s Law. This is the theoretical foundation for Koretz’s argument about score inflation, and it is worth knowing by name.

Donald Campbell wrote in 1979: the more any quantitative social indicator is used for social decision-making, the more subject it will be to corruption pressures, and the more apt it will be to distort and corrupt the social processes it is intended to monitor.

In plain language: when you attach high stakes to a number, people optimize for the number rather than for what the number is supposed to measure. Test scores go up, but learning does not, because the scores stop being valid measures of learning.

That brings us to validity. A test is valid when it measures what it claims to measure. The SAT is quite reliable — you would get a similar score on retakes — but it is not a valid measure of cooking ability. When schools narrow their curriculum to test prep, the test becomes a less valid measure of broad learning, even if it is still reliable.

There is also test bias, which is different from measurement error. A biased test is like a broken scale: it will never give the right answer, no matter how many times you use it. Modern tests work hard to eliminate this, but it is always worth asking about.

This connects directly to Koretz: when test scores become high-stakes targets, Campbell’s Law tells us the scores stop meaning what we think they mean. That is not a reason to abandon testing. It is a reason to interpret test data carefully.

Using Test Scores as a Leader

What to Ask When Someone Hands You Test Score Data

1
What type of score is this? Proficiency rate, scaled score, standardized, or growth?
2
Where’s the cut score? Who set it? Could a different threshold tell a different story?
3
What’s being compared? Snapshot (levels) or change over time (growth)?
4
What’s controlled for? Raw comparisons ignore student demographics. Has anyone adjusted?
5
Who’s missing? Are students or schools excluded from the data? Why?
You don’t need to run the analysis. You need to ask the right questions.

Bringing this together with something practical. You are going to encounter test score data constantly: in state accountability reports, program evaluations, grant applications, board presentations, news articles.

You do not need to run the analysis yourself. But you need to ask the right questions. Here are five.

One: what type of score is this? A proficiency rate, a scaled score, a standardized score, or a growth measure? Each tells a different story.

Two: where is the cut score? If someone is reporting proficiency rates, who set the threshold, and could a different threshold produce a different picture?

Three: what is being compared? A snapshot of performance at one point, or change over time? Across schools, across years, across subgroups?

Four: what is controlled for? A raw comparison of average scores across schools ignores the fact that schools serve different populations. Has anyone adjusted for demographics or baseline achievement? If not, you are comparing inputs, not school quality.

Five: who is missing? Are there students or schools excluded from the data? We talk more about this next module with survey data, but it is always worth asking.

These five questions connect to the whole sequence: score types from today, control variables from the regression modules, critical reading throughout.

Common Misinterpretations to Watch For

“Our proficiency rate went up 15 points — our teaching must be working.”
If students were near the threshold, the gain may be an artifact. If the cut score changed, it’s illusory.
“School A outperforms School B — it must be a better school.”
Without adjusting for demographics and prior achievement, you’re comparing inputs, not school quality.
“This teacher’s value-added score is low — they’re ineffective.”
VAM estimates are noisy and swing from year to year. One year of data is not enough.
“Test scores went up, so students learned more.”
Koretz: score inflation. Gains on high-stakes tests can be 3–6× larger than actual learning gains.

A few claims you will hear that should trigger your critical thinking. The first one is the opening scenario: a 15-point proficiency jump.

First: “Our proficiency rate went up 15 points — our teaching must be working.” Maybe. But if students were clustered near the threshold, that gain could be an artifact, and if the state changed its cut scores, the gain might be entirely illusory. You now have a much more specific set of questions to ask about that school than you did at the start of the lecture.

Second: “School A outperforms School B — it must be a better school.” Without adjusting for student demographics and prior achievement, that comparison tells you about inputs, not about what the school is adding. That is exactly the problem value-added tries to solve; a resource like the Educational Opportunity Project’s data explorer shows how different school rankings look by snapshot versus by growth.

Third: “This teacher’s value-added score is low — they’re ineffective.” Value-added estimates are noisy and can swing substantially from year to year with no change in teaching quality. One year of data is not enough, and ideally you want more than one year of pre-test data.

Fourth, Koretz’s core insight: “Test scores went up, so students learned more.” Not necessarily. When tests are high-stakes, schools optimize for the test, and gains on high-stakes assessments can be three to six times larger than actual gains in learning.

Each of these is something you might hear in a real meeting. Your job as a leader is to be the person who pauses and asks the right question.
