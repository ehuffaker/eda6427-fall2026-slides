The Cost-Effectiveness Ratio

Cost per unit of effect  =  cost per student  ÷  effect
A lower ratio means more gain for each dollar.
First grade fluency Cost per student Gain in fluency scores Cost per point
Literacy Groups $6,000 5 points $1,200
Literacy Afterschool $8,000 10 points $800
Their question for the district: is the extra 5 points worth the extra $2,000 per student?
Hollands & Levin (2017); fictitious programs

PlayPosit Question 1

Two middle school math programs are evaluated on the same end-of-year test with similar students. Program X costs $500 per student and raises scores by 0.25 standard deviations. Program Y costs $300 per student and raises scores by 0.10 standard deviations. Which has the lower cost per standard deviation of gain?

  1. Program Y, because it costs $200 less per student
  2. Program Y: $30 per standard deviation, compared with $125 for Program X
  3. They are equally cost-effective
  4. Program X: $2,000 per standard deviation, compared with $3,000 for Program Y
Hypothetical example.

Two Reading Programs

The same 60 first graders below the reading benchmark, the same end-of-year reading assessment.
Program Cost per student Effect Cost per SD of gain
Small-group tutoring (Module 10) $757.50 0.25 SD $3,030
Reading software $240.00 0.10 SD $2,400
The software is more cost-effective. The tutoring costs $517.50 more per student and produces 0.15 SD more.
Hypothetical programs; costs, effects, and features invented for illustration.

What the Ratio Does Not Show

1  Whether the comparison is fair
Ratios compare only when the effects are on the same outcome measure, for similar students
2  The size of the effect
The lower ratio comes with the smaller gain: 0.10 SD against 0.25 SD
3  Which students gained
One average can come from gains spread evenly or concentrated among a few students
4  The program as delivered
Certified teachers as tutors: $1,217.50 per student (Module 10). The effect may change too.

PlayPosit Question 2

Program A costs $450 per student and raised scores by 0.30 SD on the vendor’s own reading test, for all fourth graders. Program B costs $600 and raised scores by 0.20 SD on the state reading test, for fourth graders below benchmark. A colleague says A is twice as cost-effective ($1,500 vs $3,000 per SD). Best response?

  1. The ratios can’t be compared directly: the effects were measured on different tests, for different groups of students
  2. The colleague is right, because Program A’s ratio is half of Program B’s
  3. Program B is more cost-effective, because it costs more per student
  4. Multiply each ratio by the number of students before comparing them
Hypothetical example.

Other Considerations in a Program Decision

Educational goods beyond test scores
Six capacities: economic productivity, personal autonomy, democratic competence, healthy personal relationships, treating others as equals, personal fulfillment
How benefits are distributed
Every student reaching a threshold
Narrowing differences between students
Helping the students furthest behind the most
Other values
Children’s experience of school now
Parents’ interests
Respect for democratic processes
Other uses of public money
Their steps: identify the values at stake; identify the options; evaluate the options with the best available evidence; choose the best expected overall result.
Brighouse, Ladd, Loeb, & Swift (2016), optional reading

Choosing an Option Other Than the Top-Ranked One

From the article: a charter school authorizer
Of three proposed schools, School A is likely to raise achievement most for the least advantaged students, but uses “severe disciplinary mechanisms that make the daily lived experience of school a less happy one for many of the children.” School B raises achievement somewhat less and does better on other educational goods. The authors leave the choice to the authorizer.
Brighouse et al. (2016), pp. 21–22
Hypothetical: reducing fights at a high school
Adding hall monitors costs less per incident prevented than a peer mediation program. The principal chooses peer mediation, because students learn to settle conflicts themselves, a skill the incident count does not measure.
